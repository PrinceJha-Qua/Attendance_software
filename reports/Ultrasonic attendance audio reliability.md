# Engineering a 99% classroom ultrasound check-in

**A near-ultrasonic (17-20 kHz) Web Audio signal cannot, on current evidence, be assured to work 99% of the time as a standalone presence proof in Indian classrooms. A tiered system can reach 99% check-in success, with audio as the first and cheapest tier.** Nobody has published a failure rate for a deployed ultrasonic attendance product, and no study gives a "percentage of phones that work at 19 kHz" figure. The best independent replication (about 11,900 recordings, 8 systems, 5 mid/high-end phones) found heavy device-pair and room dependence, up to a 30 dB spread between phones at the same volume setting, and degradation in every scheme when the phone was in a pocket ([Putz et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02249)). Google, the largest operator, dropped ultrasonic discovery from Nearby Messages on Android in August 2021 in favour of Bluetooth ([Google](https://developers.google.com/nearby/messages/android/get-started)). The failures that matter most are correlated: a deaf microphone, an iOS PWA that loses its stream, a laptop speaker that cannot reach row 20, a pocketed phone. Repeating bursts does not fix correlated failures. The design below therefore does four things. It uses a robust low-rate spread-spectrum or chirp signal at about 17.5-19 kHz. It profiles each phone and room before trusting the channel. It binds the signal to a server-verified rotating code. It guarantees a non-acoustic completion path (on-screen code or QR, teacher override), so the claim is made on check-in success rather than on acoustic detection. The 99% claim then needs about 300 to 1,000 stratified trials, plus per-stratum floors.

Throughout, **sourced facts** carry citations. Statements labelled *Inference* are my reasoning, or the researchers' reasoning in the notes, and are not sourced. Items the notes themselves flag as unverified are marked *(unverified)*.

## Failure modes cluster in five layers, and phone capture is the weakest

### Phone capture: the microphone path is mostly undocumented

**Microphone response above 16 kHz.** There is no public per-model table of 18-20 kHz microphone attenuation. Android's compatibility definition (CDD) sets "near-ultrasound" at 18.5-20 kHz. A device that declares support must have a microphone response no more than 15 dB below its 2 kHz response, but the declaration is optional ([AOSP CDD](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/docs/source.android.com/+/20cdbb7%5E%21)). The UNPROCESSED audio source only has to stay within +-30 dB of mid-band at 7-22 kHz ([CDD 5.11 mirror](https://os.unplugged.com/werunplugged/unplugged-vendor/raw/branch/main/compatibility/cdd/5_multimedia/5_11_unprocessed-audio.md)). So passing the CDD does not guarantee a usable 19 kHz signal-to-noise ratio. SonicPACT's iPhone 7 and 8 measurements show a sharp drop above 20 kHz, with iPhone 8 attenuating from about 19 kHz, so they used 18.5 kHz ([SonicPACT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.04770)). That source was read only as a search snippet. One COVID-tracing study saw a phone speaker fall about 10 dB above 16 kHz, and more when covered ([arXiv 2008.00431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00431)). A 14-phone microphone study concluded that "most" phones could work above the audible range, but its full text was unreadable ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362094978_Frequency_Response_of_Smartphones_at_the_Upper_Limit_of_the_Audible_Range)).

**Budget phones are an unmeasured population.** All research phones in the Putz study were mid or high end. India's market is dominated by budget Android: IDC Q1 2024 put sub-$100 phones at 15% and $100-200 phones at 48% of shipments ([MySmartPrice/IDC-Counterpoint](https://www.mysmartprice.com/gear/mobiles/mobiles-news/vivo-xiaomi-samsung-drive-indias-smartphone-market-growth-in-q1-2024-counterpoint-research)). *Inference:* about 63% of shipments sat under $200, and the installed base is older and cheaper, so the least-tested hardware is the typical student phone.

**Sample rate.** The study notes report that iOS 27 beta reports 48,000 Hz for the microphone and all contexts ([WebKit 326286](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=326286)). They also report that older Safari fixed 44,100 Hz ([AddPipe](https://blog.addpipe.com/getusermedia-audio-constraints.md)). Bluetooth headset microphones fall to the HFP voice profile, which is 8 kHz (CVSD) or 16 kHz (mSBC) ([Infineon](https://community.infineon.com/t5/Wi-Fi-Bluetooth-for-Linux/How-to-find-if-the-BT-headset-supports-16khz-or-only-8khz-sampling-rate/m-p/387119)). A phone in that state cannot hear 19 kHz at all. *Inference:* at 44.1 kHz a 19 kHz tone sits only about 3 kHz below Nyquist, where anti-alias filters roll off.

**Browser voice processing.** Safari historically low-passed microphone audio near 10-13 kHz even with the three processing flags off ([WebKit 179411](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=179411)). A 2019 fix made `applyConstraints({echoCancellation:false})` on the live track the workaround. A 2023 comment says Safari no longer uses voice processing when echoCancellation is false ([WebKit 192932](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=192932)), with no confirming test. On iOS 27 beta, echoCancellation:false routes output to the quiet earpiece, and the bug is open ([WebKit 326286](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=326286)). Another report says opening the microphone with echoCancellation false still reduced output quality and forced mono ([WebKit 311451](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=311451)); the notes saw it only as a snippet. For Chrome Android, Samsung Internet and Firefox Android the notes found no current 18-19 kHz test, and no source says which AudioSource Chrome selects when processing is off. OEM audio enhancements (Samsung, Xiaomi) cannot be ruled out.

**PWA lifecycle and permissions.** iOS Home Screen web apps re-prompted for the microphone on route navigation, and the grant was not preserved across restarts, reported as persisting on iOS 16.3.1 ([WebKit 215884](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=215884), [212040](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=212040)). In one report the audio track went to "ended" about 30 seconds after the app was backgrounded, and re-calling getUserMedia on visibilitychange restored it ([WebKit 204681](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=204681)). Another reported the microphone stopping on auto-lock while speaker audio continued ([WebKit 241400](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=241400)). Whether 2026 iOS standalone PWAs still behave this way is *(unverified)*.

**Physical handling.** Pocketing degraded every scheme in the 2026 study ([Putz et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02249)). In a separate indoor-localization study, bag or pocket attenuation was large: 46% near the door versus 3% on a phone receiver ([arXiv 2002.00091](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.00091)). Hand, case and finger occlusion have no numbers in the notes (*Inference:* they attenuate 19 kHz disproportionately). Side-by-side phone placement was worse than opposite-facing.

### Emitter and room: laptops cannot cover a lecture hall

**Emitter hardware.** No measured above-16 kHz response was found for any specific laptop, projector speaker, TV or PA. The one laptop-to-laptop study measured 64.1, 64.3 and 62.9 dB(Z) at 18, 19 and 20 kHz at 20 cm and full volume, against a 48.8 dB(Z) quiet-lab noise floor, and called the band response "usually nonflat" ([arXiv 2103.11261](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.11261)). In that study 5 m worked at 100% volume (BER below 2e-4 to 0.01) but gave about 0.1 BER at 65-80% volume. Received power was lower off the device front. Nonlinear distortion appears above about 75% volume on most phones ([Putz et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02249)). Bluetooth SBC reportedly cuts content above about 14 kHz, and AAC cutoff varies by device (forum-grade claims: [Habr](https://habr.com/en/articles/456476), [SoundGuys](https://www.soundguys.com/bluetooth-audio-codecs-ultimate-guide-20296/)). Marketing "20 kHz" speaker specs are unqualified.

**Audio-chain processing.** Feedback suppressors notch as narrow as 1/80 octave and treat a sustained tone as feedback ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/dbx-afs2)). That applies to microphone channels, but a PA path shared with the lectern microphone may include one. The notes found nothing on TV enhancement modes, "clear voice" or "night mode" processing, Windows loudness equalization, HDMI downsampling in projectors or TVs, or 100 V line transformer roll-off. These are open risks for the large TV rooms and the PA hall.

**Propagation.** The researcher computed air absorption at 19 kHz from the ISO 9613-1 formula (output unverified against standard tables outside 10 kHz). At 20 C and 50% RH it is about 0.48 dB/m; at 30% RH about 0.57 dB/m. Total free-field loss versus 1 m is therefore about 16 dB at 5 m, 31 dB at 15 m and 44 dB at 30 m (about 47 dB in dry air). Absorption worsens in dry air, such as air-conditioned winter rooms. *Inference:* a laptop at about 50 dB at 1 m (derived from 64 dB at 0.2 m) would deliver roughly 6 dB at 30 m, so laptops cannot cover the 240-seat hall. A 120-seat room on projector or laptop speakers is the highest-risk configuration, because audience absorption, tweeter beaming (a 1.8 cm wavelength) and multipath fading are likely to dominate. Those effects have no measured values in the notes.

**Leakage.** With a closed door, a same-space beacon was received 87% of the time and an adjacent-space beacon 0/1,440. With the door open, 27% of messages leaked in from the other space ([arXiv 2002.00091](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.00091)). High-frequency sound is "mostly reflected by walls" ([Getreuer et al.](https://www.academia.edu/164596957/Ultrasonic_Communication_Using_Consumer_Hardware)). Open doors and corridors therefore cause both false absences and false presences. Noise from HVAC, projector fans, LED drivers and switch-mode supplies at 17-20 kHz is unmeasured in the notes.

### Signal design and detection: the technology works, in controlled settings

The robust families are well characterised. Google's protocol is 127-chip direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS) in 18.5-20 kHz at 94.5 b/s, reliable at 2 m and often at 10 m ([Getreuer et al.](https://www.academia.edu/164596957/Ultrasonic_Communication_Using_Consumer_Hardware)). Chirp spread-spectrum research reports error-free operation to 20 m at 125 bps and to 70 m at 20 bps on Android ([Shi et al. 2023](https://personalpages.surrey.ac.uk/w.wang/papers/Shi%20et%20al_PhysicalCommunication_2023.pdf)). ggwave's inaudible mode (15-19.5 kHz, 268 bps) was near-perfect up to 20 m in Putz et al.'s tests ([arXiv 2602.02249](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02249)). Replication tempers the headline numbers. Putz et al. reimplemented Nearby and found it reliable within 1 m but unreliable farther, and much worse in one office than another with the same devices. They recommend treating the channel as a low-bandwidth trigger with strong error correction and interleaving.

Failure modes here are specific:

- **Narrowband interference.** A single tone is jammed by any narrowband noise.
- **Multipath fading.** A pure tone suffers cm-scale nulls in a reverberant room.
- **Clipping.** A clipped emitter produces intermodulation products and wastes power.
- **Timing and rate mismatch.** Resampling when context and hardware rates differ adds artefacts (a 0.23% drift is reported in [Mozilla's wiki](https://wiki.mozilla.org/Special:MobileDiff/669392)). Clock offsets and Doppler from motion are small for bursts under 2 s.
- **Main-thread detection.** AnalyserNode polling is jittery. AudioWorklet runs on the audio thread ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/audio-worklet)).

*Inference:* the processing gain of a matched filter is 10 log10(TB). A 100 ms chirp over 2 kHz gives about 23 dB, and a 300 ms burst about 28 dB. That is the margin needed to offset the 20-40 dB that roll-off and distance can consume.

### Attacks: spoofing and jamming are cheap, and co-located proxy is unstoppable

Any static tone or code is defeated by a second phone, a tone-generator app or a recorded file. Beacon spoofing and injection have been demonstrated against ultrasonic ecosystems ([PoPETs 2017](https://www.petsymposium.org/popets/2017/popets-2017-0018.php)). Acoustic proximity checks cannot resist co-located attackers ([Sound-Proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.03790); [Shrestha et al.](https://spies.engr.tamu.edu/dangers-of-zero-effort-second-factor-login-based-on-ambient-audio/)). Relay through voice-call apps is limited by codec bandwidth. Opus carries 17-20 kHz only in fullband mode ([RFC 6716](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc6716)). A WhatsApp voice note analysed in 2019 was 16 kHz mono ([ffmpeg-user](https://ffmpeg.org/pipermail/ffmpeg-user/2019-July/044852.html)). Zoom "Original Sound" runs at 48 kHz with processing disabled ([Podcast Engineering School](https://podcastengineeringschool.com/new-in-zoom-high-fidelity-audio-mode)). Google Meet applies cloud denoising ([TechXplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2020-06-noise-cancellation-google.html)). Live-call behaviour for WhatsApp, Meet and Teams is *(unverified)* and needs an empirical test. *Inference:* the cheaper attack is sharing a recorded file or a screen recording, and the more worrying one is a friend carrying two phones. Emulators, virtual audio cables and microphone hijacking are undetectable acoustically, and a PWA cannot attest its device. Jamming at 17-20 kHz is easy and hits every phone in range; no jamming study of attendance systems was found.

### Human, safety and legal: manageable if bursts are short and quiet

Most young adults hear 17-18 kHz. A study of 51 adults aged 18-29 measured mean thresholds of about 17 dB at 18 kHz, on a dB scale the notes could not map to SPL ([PMC9450601](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9450601/)). A second study reportedly found 52.2% of under-30s responding at 20 kHz *(unverified; primary not opened, [PMC8394048](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8394048/))*. The ICNIRP-derived public tonal limit for the 20 kHz third-octave band is reported as about 70 dB SPL *(via summaries of Leighton's papers, unverified against ICNIRP primary)*, and Leighton notes the evidence base is thin and old ([Southampton](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/421979)). Symptoms such as nausea and headache are anecdotal. Animals hear this band, and cats, dogs and rodents extend well above 20 kHz ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/613488)). Hearing aids and cochlear implants process roughly up to 8 kHz ([JAAA 2015](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.3766/jaaa.26.2.3.pdf)). Hyperacusis is common in autism (50-70% in one review), but nothing ties it to 17-20 kHz.

On the legal side, India's DPDP Rules 2025 (notified November 2025) require plain-language notice and free, specific, informed consent ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf)). The children's-data carve-out for educational institutions is of unclear scope, and some first-years may be 17. The FTC's 2015-16 warning letters over undisclosed ultrasonic beaconing show the reputational risk ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/silverpush-ftc-stop-eavesdropping-with-audio-beacons/)). Whether a university qualifies for any DPDP exemption, and how Indian law treats ambient sensing, were not resolved and need counsel.

## What is and is not known

The notes support four things firmly. Near-ultrasound tokens can be delivered to commodity phones at metres to tens of metres under good conditions. Device and room variance is large. Browser processing has historically destroyed the band, especially on Safari. Spoofing needs a server-side rotating code. They do not support any of the following:

| Question | Status |
|---|---|
| Share of phones (especially Indian budget Android) that hear 18-19 kHz | No data; the only device-count study is unreadable |
| Share of laptops, projectors, TVs, PA that emit 18-19 kHz at useful SPL | No per-model data; one laptop study only |
| Current (2025-26) iOS 26/27 Safari and Chrome Android raw-microphone bandwidth | Not measured; Safari fix claims lack confirming tests |
| iOS standalone PWA microphone persistence in 2026 | Unverified |
| Success rate of CourseKey, Lisnr, Tez audio QR or any attendance product | Not published; CourseKey claims are press release and patent only |
| Why Google dropped ultrasound from Nearby | Not stated; "radio judged more reliable" is inference |
| Classroom 17-20 kHz noise floors; wall, door, seat absorption | Not measured |
| Relay through WhatsApp, Meet, Teams live calls | Unverified; needs an empirical test |
| Audibility percentages of 17, 18, 19 kHz in free field at given SPL; ICNIRP exact limit | Not cleanly sourced |
| Interaction with hearing aids, tinnitus, hyperacusis at these frequencies | No evidence either way |
| DPDP applicability to universities | Unresolved; counsel needed |

Three further cautions apply. The notes' vendor claims (CourseKey's 90+ campuses and 2M+ check-ins across all methods, Lisnr's 100M devices) are marketing and say nothing about acoustic reliability. The Sound-Proof results (EER 0.002 over 2,007 logins, [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1503.03790)) are for ambient audio below 4 kHz, not ultrasound, so they do not transfer. Zero hits for forum or Reddit complaints reflects search limits, not an absence of complaints. A Hacker News anecdote that a MacBook falls off sharply near 14 kHz is uncontrolled ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=6647590)).

## A tiered design that makes 99% a property of check-in, not of acoustics

The design principle is that the 99% claim attaches to *the student's check-in succeeding within the session window*. Acoustic detection is tier 1. *Everything in this section is Inference, informed by the sourced facts above, unless a citation is given.*

**Band and modulation.** Use a linear-chirp preamble for synchronisation and channel estimation, followed by a spread-spectrum payload (DSSS or chirp spread spectrum, CSS) at about 50-100 b/s. The preamble should span roughly 17.5-19.5 kHz. The payload carries a 64-bit frame: session ID, counter and CRC-16, with a short convolutional or Reed-Solomon code. Keep the primary band at about 17.5-19 kHz; the evidence on iPhone 7/8 and the 44.1 kHz anti-alias skirt argues against 19.5 kHz and above. Alternate bursts between two or three frequency slots (for example about 17.5, 18.5 and 19.5 kHz) or use a full-band sweep so that any usable sub-band contributes. Use raised-cosine edges to avoid audible clicks, and set the emitter level about -12 dBFS to avoid clipping and intermodulation. Send 1.2-1.5 s bursts roughly every 3 s. A continuous steady tone is a poor choice: it is more annoying, jammable, and a feedback suppressor will notch it.

**Redundancy that counts.** If bursts failed independently with p = 0.8, five bursts would give 99.97% (the notes' own arithmetic, 1-(1-p)^N). That arithmetic does not hold for correlated failures, and those dominate (deaf microphone, pocket, rear row behind the audience). Redundancy therefore has three layers: time repetition across a 30-60 s window, frequency diversity, and emitter diversity. In the 240-seat hall use two or more distributed emitters no more than 10-15 m apart, each with a distinct code or time slot to avoid destructive nulls.

**Emitter rules.** Prefer a fixed room PC on a wired path (HDMI, USB or analog) at 48 kHz; treat Bluetooth SBC and AAC as failing. Inject the beacon after any limiter and bypass any feedback suppressor, using a separate zone or amp if necessary. For the 240-seat PA hall that means a dedicated feed or tweeter. For the 120-seat room and ordinary classrooms with TVs, assume built-in speakers fail until a room survey proves otherwise, and plan on a cheap dedicated powered tweeter or USB speaker with known 19 kHz output at about 85-95 dB at 1 m. Use a fixed placement aimed at the audience, mounted high.

**Receiver.** Request `echoCancellation:false, noiseSuppression:false, autoGainControl:false, channelCount:1`, then read `track.getSettings()` and, on Safari, call `applyConstraints` after acquisition. Run detection in an AudioWorklet by matched-filter correlation after decimation to baseband, with a CFAR threshold from a rolling median and MAD of correlation magnitude. Accept on CRC plus agreement of 2 of N bursts. Start the AudioContext and microphone from a user gesture on a single page without navigation. Listen for track `ended` and `mute`, `visibilitychange` and context `statechange`, and re-acquire. Run the microphone only inside a teacher-started window, with a visible indicator.

**Self-test and capability profile.** Before trusting the channel, each phone and room is profiled:

1. Read the settings. Fail the audio tier if the sample rate is under 44.1 kHz or the device label looks like Bluetooth.
2. Run a 2 s noise-floor probe and compare 17-20 kHz energy against 1-4 kHz and 10-15 kHz, looking for a cliff or mirrored energy, the signature of old Safari.
3. Use the teacher or reference device to play calibration chirps or pilot tones at about 15, 17, 18.5 and 19.5 kHz at session start; each phone reports per-tone SNR, and the highest tone with at least 10-15 dB SNR is accepted. Show a live "hearing the room" indicator. This also surfaces pocketed or covered phones.
4. Log device model, browser, tier and SNR to the server for the fleet map and for stratified analysis.

The room profile uses the same pilots from the emitter side: measure a dB map on a grid (every 2-3 rows by 3-4 seats, plus corners, back and sides) with students seated, HVAC on, and the door both closed and open. Require at least 10 dB SNR at the worst seat. Do this once per room, and again after any hardware change.

**Tiers.**

| Tier | Mechanism | Used when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chirp/DSSS 17.5-19.5 kHz, automatic check-in | Self-test passes (target majority of phones) |
| 2 | Lower band 16-18 kHz, longer integration | Tier 1 SNR low but 16-18 kHz passes; faintly audible to some, so use the shortest bursts and quietest level that works |
| 3 | Non-acoustic: teacher-displayed rotating short code or QR on the projector/TV, entered or scanned in the PWA | Audio tiers fail, microphone denied, session degraded |
| 4 | Teacher or admin override, with logged reason | Device broken, student without a phone; and a session-wide degradation mode |

The code on screen in tier 3 is a *rotating HMAC code*, not a static word. *Note:* BLE is not available to a Safari PWA, so it cannot be the universal fallback on iOS. If a majority of phones fail the self-test at once, mark the session degraded and do not penalise students; this also covers jamming.

**Server verification.** The emitted code is HMAC(secret, session ID, time slot), accepted for the current and previous slot only. The phone submits the decoded code plus SNR, tier and timestamp. The server checks the slot, rejects duplicates, and compares signal-strength profiles across phones in the same room to flag outliers (a clean signal with none of the room's reverb or noise). Add a reverse path where practical, so that the student's phone is itself heard by the teacher device or neighbours. Time-of-flight is too coarse for per-seat checks because phone audio-latency jitter is tens of milliseconds. Use random spot-checks and anomaly analytics for the two-phone proxy problem. Treat the audio code as presence evidence, not proof: *Inference from* the Braunschweig authors' note that beacons are spoofable ([Infosecurity](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/android-apps-with-ultrasonic/)) *and from the record-and-playback literature.* A native app with Play Integrity or App Attest would add device attestation and background capture, but it is outside this PWA scope.

**Safety and privacy.** Keep burst duty cycle low and keep level at the minimum that clears the worst-seat margin. Target under about 60-65 dB SPL at 1 m and at least 5 dB below the 70 dB public tonal limit at any seat (engineering judgement; the 70 dB figure is unverified). Measure with a microphone that is calibrated above 20 kHz. Audible clicks and intermodulation, not the tone itself, are the likelier source of complaints. Exclude rooms near animal facilities. Offer a no-questions opt-out into tier 3 or 4. For the "not recording" claim: high-pass or band-limit on device so speech is physically absent, hold frames in RAM only, send off-device only the decoded code, boolean and SNR, publish a data-flow diagram, and use a short, honest permission string. Provide a DPDP notice and a grievance contact. Counsel should confirm the legal basis; the notes did not.

## A defensible 99% claim needs stratified trials and honest bounds

**Define the unit.** The primary claim is: *the probability that a student present in a session completes check-in within the window, by any tier, is at least 99%.* Report separately (a) tier-1 acoustic success, (b) the share falling through to each tier, and (c) false accept and false reject of the verification layer. A claim that rolls fallbacks into acoustic reliability would mislead, so label it.

**Sample size arithmetic.** I computed exact binomial bounds. With zero failures, **299 independent trials** demonstrate failure below 1% at 95% one-sided confidence (the rule of three; [Quality Digest](https://www.qualitydigest.com/inside/statistics-column/rules-three-and-five-082317.html)). About 461 are needed at 99% confidence ([Turkiye Klinikleri](https://turkiyeklinikleri.com/article/en-tedavide-basarisizlik-olasiligi-icin-ust-sinir-belirleme-yontemlerinin-karsilastirilmasi-metodolojik-bir-calisma-109311.html)). Allowing failures, the Clopper-Pearson requirement at 95% for a lower bound of 99% is:

| Failures allowed | Trials needed |
|---|---|
| 0 | 299 |
| 1 | 473 |
| 2 | 628 |
| 3 | 773 |
| 5 | 1,049 |

A pooled pass says little about a minority device, so the design needs per-stratum floors as well. With 60 trials and zero failures in a stratum the 95% lower bound is only 95.1%, and with 30 trials it is 90.5%; per-stratum results are therefore reported as bounds, not as 99% claims. Pooled claims weight strata by expected fleet share.

**Stratification.** Cross these factors, sampling the cells where risk concentrates rather than the full product:

- **Devices:** 15-25 phone models weighted to the Indian fleet (Vivo, Xiaomi/Poco, Samsung, Oppo/OnePlus, Realme from the Canalys shares, [Canalys](https://canalys.com/newsroom/india-smartphone-shipments-Q1-2024)), including sub-$100 and $100-200 phones, older iPhones and current iPhones. Each model needs at least 20-30 check-ins in each of at least two browsers, with iOS standalone PWA tested separately from Safari tab.
- **Rooms and emitters:** each real room type (120-seat with projector speakers, 120-seat with laptop speakers, 240-seat PA, ordinary TV room), at least 3 instances each where possible.
- **Seats:** front, middle, rear, sides and corners, with a full house of seated students (audience absorption).
- **Handling:** in hand, on desk, in pocket, in bag, under a case, face down.
- **Conditions:** HVAC and fans on and off, door open and closed, dry versus humid season, normal versus loud ambient.
- **Time:** repeat across days and weeks, and across the hardware lifecycle.

**Analysis.** Treat students-in-a-session as clustered, not independent, because room, emitter and time-of-day effects are shared. The effective sample size is smaller than the raw count. Estimate the intraclass correlation from a pilot and inflate the targets by the design effect 1 + (m - 1) * ICC for cluster size m. Report Clopper-Pearson (exact) bounds for each stratum and a cluster-bootstrap or mixed-effects bound for the pooled figure. Set the acceptance rule in advance: the one-sided 95% lower bound on end-to-end success at least 99% overall, tier-1 coverage and fallback rates reported, and the worst stratum above an agreed floor (for example 95% lower bound at 90%, after which the failing strata get a design remedy, usually a dedicated emitter or a forced tier 3). Run it as three stages: a lab device matrix (phone by browser, controlled distance and angle), a room survey for the emitter dB maps, then a live pilot with real classes. Re-run the matrix on browser or OS updates, because Safari behaviour has changed repeatedly. Add pilot checks for audibility with students (including a discomfort and opt-out survey), the relay tests on WhatsApp, Meet, Teams and Zoom (play a 19 kHz tone through each app between two phones and analyse the far end), and hearing-aid and implant users if any volunteer.

**Alternative: when 99% is out of reach acoustically.** If the survey shows a room's worst-seat margin under 10 dB, or a device stratum cannot pass the self-test, do not stretch the acoustic claim. Route that room or stratum to tier 3 by default and report the acoustic share honestly. The end-to-end claim still holds, because the fallback is deterministic.

## Conclusion

The central finding is that the question changes shape once failures are sorted by correlation. The signal-design literature, with matched filtering, spread spectrum and repetition, can push single-device detection very high in a good channel, but the failures that decide a 99% target (an unmeasured budget microphone, a lost iOS PWA stream, a laptop speaker that cannot reach row 20, a pocketed phone) are properties of devices and rooms. They are not noise, and repetition cannot average them away. The practical consequence is that the capability self-test, per-room survey and non-acoustic tier are the reliability mechanism. The chirp or DSSS waveform is the smaller part.

The most valuable next steps are inexpensive and specific. Run a 20-phone, three-browser pilot on real Indian budget handsets to get the first honest "percentage that hears 18-19 kHz". Do an emitter dB map in the actual 120-seat and 240-seat rooms. Test relay over live calls, and confirm the iOS PWA lifecycle on current iOS versions. Treat any vendor or paper figure for classroom reliability as a hypothesis until those measurements exist, since no published number is yet available for this population and these rooms.
